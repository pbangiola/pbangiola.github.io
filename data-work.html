Data work

I’ve worked with student data as a teacher, a curriculum director, and an edtech implementer. In each job, I needed the data to help somebody do something: get a learner into a course, figure out what a class needed, or decide whether a change was working.

Some of that work involved Python and R. Some involved Excel, permissions, or sitting down with a teacher to work through what a result actually meant. I’m interested in the whole problem, including the parts that happen before there’s anything worth putting on a graph.

The projects I build now, including NextTask, give me another way to work on those problems. A task list and a student information system serve very different purposes, but both become difficult to use when the relationships between their records stop making sense.

The work examples below use aggregate results and general descriptions of the organizations. Individual student records, client files, and internal documents aren’t published here. Code images come from my public NextTask repository.

Education technology in correctional institutions

Before anyone could learn, their accounts had to work.

When you deliver educational software in a correctional institution, getting a learner into a course involves more than handing over a tablet. The learner needs the right accounts, the accounts need to work across the services they’re using, and the people supporting them need a process they can actually follow. Different institutions also have different requirements.

I built an account-provisioning process, maintained a data warehouse across jurisdictions, and developed standards for how we handled learner information, including personally identifying information. I used R, Python, Excel, and Google Drive to prototype, deploy, and evaluate the work. I also worked with engineering on provisioning and system stability.

There were practical details that mattered just as much as the underlying data. Learners needed a way to find the passwords for their different services in one place. Clients needed useful reporting. Support associates needed training, and outsourced technical support needed scripts that reflected the problems people were actually running into.

The onboarding system supported thousands of learners daily with a 24-hour turnaround. The revised implementation process was repeated across 19 implementations. That last part mattered to me: other people had to be able to run it, and it had to keep working as the work expanded.

A process that works once is a start. A process that other people can repeat is something you can build on.
A note on the two reliability measures

My records report 99.9% fidelity for a human-mediated process and 99.7% effectiveness for a prototype. Those are two different statistics. They describe different work, and I’ve kept them separate rather than drawing a comparison that the underlying measures don’t support. Account counts and learner counts are also distinct.

K–8 curriculum and school leadership

What would tell us whether the school was improving?

At the K–8 school where I led curriculum, I used nationally normed diagnostic results to follow student achievement and growth over multiple years. I looked across student groups and used that information in decisions about curriculum, interventions, and access to services.

Achievement and growth answer different questions. A student can be ahead of the national comparison group and still be making very little progress. Another can be behind and learning quickly. If we only look at where students finished, we miss part of what happened during the year.

The schoolwide results below show both views. Average math achievement moved from the 52nd to the 68th percentile, and reading from the 61st to the 70th. Median annual growth also increased: from 87% to 119% of the national average in math, and from 114% to 143% in reading.

The analysis was part of a larger job. I led changes in math and foundational literacy, implemented a multi-tiered system of supports, helped obtain IEP and 504 documentation for students who needed it, and worked with teachers on instruction. The numbers helped me decide where to look and what to ask. The next step was often a conversation about materials, a lesson, or a student who needed something different.

I also administered PowerSchool and Google Workspace, including provisioning and permissions. That meant working on both sides of the problem: keeping the systems useful and making sense of the information they held.

Ninth-grade English

Sometimes the wrong answer tells you what to teach.

One of my more useful experiences with assessment data came while teaching ninth-grade grammar. The materials I was given were written as though my students simply didn’t have a command of English. But as I looked at the questions they missed, I saw particular patterns. Some involved differences between the grammar of their home dialect and the conventions expected on the test.

That matters. If a student already uses a consistent grammatical system, explaining a different system as though it’s the only one can make the lesson both confusing and insulting. I started teaching those differences explicitly. I wanted students to understand what the test expected, why that answer differed from something they might say at home, and how to give the answer that would support their future.

My students gained an average of four points on the 36-point pre-ACT scale over the year. I wouldn’t claim that teaching language variation alone explains that result. It was part of an original grammar and writing curriculum that I continued to improve as I learned more about what students understood.

I also developed Excel tools and trained colleagues to make recurring assessment analysis less time-consuming, saving approximately three hours a week. That gave us more time to do the part that required a teacher: looking carefully at what students were doing and deciding how to respond.

The useful question was what the answer revealed about the student’s thinking, and what I should do with that information.

If I taught this again, I’d use some of the explanations I found later in the year much earlier. It was a delicate topic. Helping students understand why I was bringing up language differences, and what I meant by them, was part of the work.

An ongoing personal project

How this connects to NextTask

NextTask is a task-prioritization and focus-timer app I’m building. You sort what matters, choose work, and keep track of the time you spend on it. As it has grown to include projects and subtasks, it has also become a useful place to work through questions about how records relate to each other.

Consider student assessment data. A score needs to stay attached to the right student, but it also needs its date and assessment context. Two scores are only useful as a comparison if you know what each one represents. In NextTask, a task needs to stay attached to the right project, and the project total needs to reflect the work that belongs to it.

Education software support

The information also has to be findable.

At an education software provider, I took over a knowledge-management project that had stalled. The initial assignment covered one product. It grew to five, including customer-specific variations, and I produced, organized, and uploaded 200 pages of documentation in two months.

The knowledge base supported technical support for teachers across 15 states. I also worked to make the process sustainable with the staff already there. A useful set of documents needs a way to stay useful as the products change.

This connects to the rest of the work in a fairly practical way. Someone trying to solve a problem needs to find the relevant information, understand which situation it applies to, and know what to do next. That’s true whether they’re a support associate looking up a product variation, a teacher looking at assessment results, or someone opening NextTask to decide what to work on.

What I’d like to show next

I’m continuing to build out this part of the site. Public code gives me one way to show the mechanics. I’d also like to add more worked examples of how I organize a question, examine the evidence, and decide what to change, using material I can share without exposing the people behind it.

Across these projects I’ve used Python, R, Excel, Google Sheets, Tableau, Google Workspace, and PowerSchool. The tool depends on the job. Sometimes the most useful thing I can make is a script; sometimes it’s a spreadsheet that another teacher can use without me sitting next to them.
